Chapter 2: Cell: The Building Block of Life

2.1 How to Study Cells?

This section introduces the concept of the limit of resolution of the human eye, which is 0.1 mm, and explains why cells cannot be seen with the naked eye. It establishes that microscopes overcome this limitation by using combinations of lenses (objective lens and eyepiece) to magnify objects. Robert Hooke is credited with the first observation of cells in 1665 using a self-designed microscope, when he observed box-like compartments in a thin slice of cork and named them "cells." The section includes Activity 2.1 (student-executed), in which students estimate the actual size of a cell by measuring the diameter of the microscope's field of view and counting the number of onion peel cells along that diameter, using a formula to compute estimated cell size. It closes by noting that scientists have improved microscopes along three dimensions — resolution, contrast, and magnification — to make them powerful tools for cell study.

2.2 Structure of a Cell

This introductory section establishes that cells function as units by interacting with one another and with their surroundings, and that these interactions occur at the cell boundary. It explains that even single-celled organisms exchange materials and respond to their environment through the cell membrane. The section frames the two subsections that follow by posing the central question of how the cell boundary controls what moves in and out of the cell.

2.2.1 Cell membrane — The universal feature of a cell

The cell membrane (also called the plasma membrane) is described as a selectively permeable, thin boundary that surrounds a cell and defines its individuality. The section introduces osmosis — the movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane from a dilute solution (more water, less solute) to a concentrated solution (less water, more solute) — and distinguishes it from diffusion, which is the net movement of any particles from higher to lower concentration without requiring a membrane. Activity 2.2 (student-executed) demonstrates osmosis by placing potato pieces in plain water (piece swells) and a 20 per cent salt or sugar solution (piece shrinks), allowing students to infer selective permeability. Structurally, the cell membrane is 7–10 nm thick and composed of lipids and proteins; the fluid-mosaic model describes it as a lipid bilayer with embedded proteins that act as gatekeepers, with molecules able to move, flip, and rotate within the membrane.

2.2.2 Cell wall — The outer covering of cells

The cell wall is an additional rigid layer present outside the cell membrane in plant, fungal, and bacterial cells, but absent in animal cells. In plants, it is primarily made of cellulose and is permeable to water and dissolved minerals, helping plant roots absorb nutrients; it provides structural support, keeps leaves and flowers firm, and maintains cell shape even when the cell loses water through osmosis. Activity 2.3 (student-executed) requires students to prepare and observe slides of onion/Rhoeo leaf peel cells and human cheek cells under a microscope, and then to place these in a 20 per cent sugar solution — the observation that plant cells retain their outer boundary while their inner content shrinks (plasmolysis) demonstrates that the rigid cell wall maintains shape, whereas animal cells (cheek cells) shrink considerably because they lack this protection. The section notes that animal cells' flexibility without a cell wall supports movement and functioning of animal tissues.

2.3 The Cell Interior — A Coordinated Working System

This section establishes that most cells share three basic components: a selectively permeable plasma membrane, a semi-fluid cytoplasm, and a prominent nucleus. It further notes that the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells contains several sub-cellular components called organelles, most of which are visible only under an electron microscope. Activity 2.4 (student study exercise — not a physical lab activity) asks students to compare diagrams of bacterial, plant, and animal cells and record which structures are present in each. The section introduces the distinction between prokaryotic cells (e.g., bacteria), which lack a well-defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles and carry out cellular activities directly in the cytoplasm, and eukaryotic cells (plant and animal cells), which possess a true nucleus and several membrane-bound organelles. Comparative data are presented in Tables 2.1 and 2.2, distinguishing the two cell types across characteristics such as typical diameter (1–10 µm for prokaryotes; 10–100 µm for eukaryotes), presence of membrane-bound organelles, and unicellular vs. multicellular organisation.

2.3.1 Why do eukaryotic cells need these organelles?

This section explains that eukaryotic cells carry out multiple life processes simultaneously in different organelles, and that these organelles function collectively like a tiny living factory — building new materials, removing waste, and providing energy.

Nucleus — House of coded instructions

The nucleus is enclosed by a double-layered nuclear membrane with pores that allow transfer of material between the nucleus and cytoplasm. The nucleolus, a dense round body within the nucleus, is the site of ribosomal subunit synthesis; these subunits exit to the cytoplasm where they assemble into ribosomes. Chromosomes — the rod-shaped carriers of genetic information composed of DNA and specific proteins — are visible only when the cell is about to divide; in non-dividing cells, the genetic material exists as chromatin (an entangled mass of thread-like structures). The functional segments of DNA are called genes, and each chromosome contains one DNA molecule that encodes hereditary information passed from parents to offspring. In prokaryotic cells, the DNA is a single circular molecule and the region containing it is called the nucleoid.

Ribosomes — The protein factories

Ribosomes are tiny structures found either freely in the cytoplasm or attached to the endoplasmic reticulum, and they are the sites of protein synthesis in the cell. They are assembled from subunits produced in the nucleolus.

Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) — Manufacturing factory

The Endoplasmic Reticulum is a large network-like organelle that spreads through the cytoplasm and is continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It plays a key role in the synthesis and transport of proteins, fats (lipids), and some hormones in specialised cells. There are two types: Rough ER (RER), which has ribosomes on its surface and is primarily involved in protein synthesis and secretion (e.g., in pancreatic cells), and Smooth ER (SER), which lacks ribosomes and is involved in the synthesis and storage of fats and hormones.

Golgi apparatus — The packaging and shipping centres

The Golgi apparatus consists of stacks of flattened, sac-like structures (cisternae) and is functionally linked to the ER, cell membrane, and other organelles. It acts as the cell's post office — modifying, sorting, and packaging proteins and/or lipids into vesicles for transport, secretion, or lysosome formation. The organelle was first observed in 1898 by Camillo Golgi in nerve cells.

Lysosomes — The clean-up system

Lysosomes are single membrane-bound sacs filled with digestive enzymes that break down unwanted proteins, carbohydrates, fats, and damaged parts of the cell. The breakdown products are released into the cytoplasm where they may be reused in other cellular processes, keeping the cell clean and healthy.

Mitochondria — The powerhouse of the cell

Mitochondria are double-membrane-bound organelles: the outer membrane is smooth and porous, while the inner membrane is folded into finger-like projections called cristae, which increase the surface area for chemical reactions involved in energy production. Through a process called cellular respiration, glucose and other molecules are broken down in the mitochondria to release energy, which is stored as ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) — the energy currency of the cell. Mitochondria possess their own DNA and ribosomes, suggesting an evolutionary relationship with certain bacteria.

Plastids — Centre for food synthesis in the plant cells and beyond

Plastids are organelles found in plant cells that are specialised for food synthesis and storage. Chloroplasts are double-membrane-bound plastids containing chlorophyll (a green pigment) in the stroma; they absorb sunlight to carry out photosynthesis, and the sugars produced are stored in the stroma along with starch granules. Like mitochondria, chloroplasts have their own DNA and ribosomes. The section notes that plastids and mitochondria share evolutionary characteristics with certain bacteria.

How do flowers, fruits, and vegetables acquire varied colours?

Chromoplasts are plastids in flower petals and fruits that contain non-chlorophyll pigments (yellow, orange, or red), giving these organs their bright colours which attract pollinators and seed-dispersing animals. Leucoplasts are colourless plastids that store food material such as starch (e.g., in potato and taro cells), oils, or proteins; they are classified based on the type of food stored.

Vacuoles — The organelles for storage and support

In mature plant cells, a large central vacuole filled with cell sap (a watery fluid) stores water, minerals, sugars, and waste materials. By maintaining water pressure (turgor), the vacuole keeps plant cells firm; when a plant lacks water, the vacuole shrinks, cells lose firmness, and the plant wilts. Animal cells may contain smaller vacuoles that temporarily store materials, though they are not as prominent as plant vacuoles.

2.4 How do Normal Cells Grow and Divide?

This section introduces cell division as the process by which organisms grow, repair damaged tissues, and replace old or dead cells — body growth occurs not because cells get bigger but because cells divide to form new cells. Activity 2.5 (student-executed) requires students to grow onion root tips in water for 5–6 days, fix the roots in aceto-alcohol, stain with aceto-carmine, squash and observe under a microscope; the different structural appearances of cells in the root tip correspond to different stages of cell division. The section notes that eukaryotic cells divide in a controlled and orderly manner through a regulated process called the cell cycle, the stages of which will be studied in higher grades.

2.4.1 Cell division

Cell division is the process by which new cells are formed from pre-existing cells, enabling growth, tissue repair, and reproduction. The two major types are mitosis and meiosis.

Mitosis

Mitosis is the most common type of cell division, in which one parent cell divides to produce two genetically identical daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent. It is responsible for normal growth, repair, maintenance, and asexual reproduction.

Meiosis

Meiosis is a two-step division process that occurs only in the cells of reproductive organs and produces gametes. In the first division, the chromosome number is halved; the second division is similar to mitosis, resulting in four daughter cells each with half the number of chromosomes (haploid). In animals, meiosis occurs in the testes (producing sperm) and ovaries (producing eggs); in plants, it occurs in anthers (producing pollen) and ovaries (producing egg cells). During fertilisation, gametes from two individuals combine to restore the original chromosome number, enabling genetic diversity among offspring. Errors in meiosis can lead to genetic disorders, reduced fertility, or early pregnancy loss; errors in mitosis can cause uncontrolled cell division, tumour formation, and abnormal chromosome numbers.

2.5 Cell Theory — The Unifying Principle of Biology

This section presents the historical development of Cell Theory: Matthias Schleiden (1838) reported all plants are made of cells; Theodor Schwann (1839) extended this to animals; and Rudolf Virchow (1855) added that new cells arise only from pre-existing cells. The three tenets of classical Cell Theory are: (1) all living organisms are made up of one or more cells; (2) the cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living beings; and (3) all cells arise from pre-existing cells. This theory unifies all of biology and explains life's continuity through cell division.

2.5.1 Do cells grow and reproduce forever?

Every cell has a definite lifespan — cells grow, carry out their functions, and eventually die, to be replaced by new cells performing the same function. In many animal cells, cell division stops when cells come into contact with neighbouring cells (contact inhibition); cancer cells lose this control and divide uncontrollably, forming tumours. Plant cells, due to their rigid cell walls, do not exhibit contact inhibition and follow a different pattern of growth. The section also notes the concept of Programmed Cell Death (PCD) — a genetically regulated, organised process of selective cell destruction essential for normal development (e.g., forming fingers in an embryo by eliminating cells between digits), immune function, and cellular quality control.

The chapter also connects its science to applied and societal contexts through a Bridging Science and Society box on cell culture — the technique of growing plant and animal cells outside the body in a nutrient-rich medium under controlled conditions of temperature, pH, and sterility. Cell culture is described as crucial for studying cell function and for producing biochemicals, food, medicines, and vaccines, highlighting the direct link between cell biology knowledge and biotechnological applications.
