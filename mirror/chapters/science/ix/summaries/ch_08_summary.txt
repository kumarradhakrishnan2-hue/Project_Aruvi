Chapter 8: Journey Inside the Atom

Section 8.1 introduces the historical and philosophical roots of atomic theory, tracing the idea back more than 2,000 years to ancient India and ancient Greece. In India, Acharya Kanada proposed that matter (dravya), if divided repeatedly, would yield the smallest indivisible particles he called parmanus, whose combinations form all material things; his ideas are recorded in the Vaisesika Sutras. The Greek philosophers Leucippus and Democritus independently proposed similar indivisible particles called atomos. The section emphasises that both concepts arose as philosophical ideas rather than from experiment, and that many centuries later, in 1808, John Dalton proposed the first scientific atomic theory, asserting that all matter is composed of indivisible atoms — a starting point for understanding atomic structure.

Section 8.2 sets the stage for tracing how atomic models evolved over more than a century, driven by new experimental evidence. It establishes that atoms were believed to be indivisible until late 19th-century scientists discovered that certain elements emit radiation, revealing that atoms themselves must contain smaller constituents. In 1897, J. J. Thomson studied cathode rays — streams produced in a low-pressure glass tube under high voltage — and identified them as negatively charged particles much lighter than atoms, which he named electrons. The nature of cathode rays was found to be independent of the cathode material or the gas in the tube, establishing electrons as a fundamental component of every atom; by convention, the electron's charge is taken as −1.

Section 8.2.1 describes Thomson's response to his own discovery: since electrons carry negative charge yet atoms are neutral, he proposed the plum pudding (or watermelon) model, in which the atom is a sphere of uniformly distributed positive charge with electrons embedded throughout it. The red pulp of a watermelon represents the positive matter and the seeds represent the electrons. This was the first genuine attempt to describe how positive and negative charges coexist in a neutral atom, though it was later superseded.

Section 8.2.2 describes how Geiger and Marsden, working under Rutherford in 1911, tested Thomson's model by directing a narrow beam of positively charged alpha particles at an extremely thin gold foil. Thomson's model predicted only slight deflections, but the experiment yielded a surprising result: while most alpha particles passed straight through, some were sharply deflected and a few even bounced back — a phenomenon called scattering, giving the experiment its alternative name, the alpha-ray scattering experiment. Thomson's model could not account for these large-angle deflections or for the fact that most particles passed undeflected, and was therefore abandoned.

Named content-block A describes Rutherford's nuclear model of the atom, derived from the gold foil results. Rutherford concluded that the positive charge and most of the mass of an atom are concentrated in an extremely small, dense region called the nucleus, while the rest of the atom is mostly empty space (explaining why most alpha particles passed through undeflected). Electrons were proposed to revolve around the nucleus like planets orbiting the Sun, giving this the name the planetary model. The nucleus is about 10⁵ times smaller than the atom: the atomic diameter is approximately 10⁻¹⁰ m while the nuclear diameter is approximately 10⁻¹⁵ m.

Named content-block B explains the key limitation of Rutherford's model: it could not account for atomic stability. Classical physics predicts that an accelerating charged particle must radiate energy; an electron in circular motion is continuously accelerating, so it should lose energy, spiral inward, and collapse into the nucleus. Since atoms are observably stable, Rutherford's model was incomplete and a new explanation was needed.

Named content-block C introduces the proton, discovered and named by Rutherford. Protons carry positive charge equal and opposite to that of the electron, and are much heavier than electrons. Since atoms are electrically neutral, the number of protons equals the number of electrons; for example, helium has 2 protons and 2 electrons, sodium has 11 protons and 11 electrons.

Section 8.2.3 describes Niels Bohr's 1913 model, proposed to resolve the stability problem. Bohr postulated that electrons occupy fixed circular paths called stationary states, orbits, or shells (also called energy levels), designated K, L, M, N… or n = 1, 2, 3, 4…; electrons moving in these fixed shells do not lose energy. The K-shell (n = 1) is closest to the nucleus and has the least energy; energy increases with distance from the nucleus. An electron can move between shells only by absorbing or releasing a discrete amount of energy equal to the difference between the two levels, and each shell can hold a limited number of electrons. Bohr's model explained atomic stability and matched many experimental observations, though it was later refined by the quantum mechanical model.

Section 8.3 identifies the puzzle that arose from comparing hydrogen and helium: helium has two protons yet its mass is about four times that of hydrogen, not twice. This indicated the presence of additional mass-contributing particles in the nucleus that carry no charge.

Section 8.3.1 describes how James Chadwick solved this in 1932 by discovering the neutron — a subatomic particle with mass nearly equal to a proton but with no electrical charge, represented by the symbol n⁰. Neutrons are found in the nucleus of all atoms except hydrogen. Together, protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons) account for almost all of an atom's mass; Table 8.1 lists the symbols and relative charges of the three subatomic particles. Heavier atoms require more neutrons than protons to maintain nuclear stability, because neutrons help reduce proton-proton repulsion and strengthen the nuclear force.

Section 8.4 traces the history of element symbols from Dalton's pictorial symbols (1803) through Berzelius's suggestion (1813) that symbols derive from Latin names, to the current IUPAC system. The conventions include: symbols are one or two letters; the first letter is capitalised, the second (if any) is lowercase; some symbols derive from Latin, Greek, or German names (e.g. Fe from Latin ferrum for iron, Hg from Greek hydrargyros for mercury). A reference table of common elements and their symbols is provided. Scientists use these internationally recognised symbols to communicate across language barriers.

Section 8.5 defines the atomic number (symbol Z) as the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of an element. Since atoms are neutral, atomic number also equals the number of electrons; for example, hydrogen has Z = 1 and helium has Z = 2. The atomic number uniquely identifies an element and determines its chemical behaviour — elements with different atomic numbers are chemically distinct.

Section 8.6 defines the mass number (symbol A) as the total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus. The formula is A = number of protons + number of neutrons. Electrons have negligible mass and are excluded from this count. Table 8.3 gives examples: hydrogen (1 proton, 0 neutrons, A = 1), helium (2 protons, 2 neutrons, A = 4), lithium (3 protons, 4 neutrons, A = 7). The standard notation writes the mass number superscript and atomic number subscript to the left of the element symbol (e.g. ¹²₆C for carbon-12). The protons and neutrons in the nucleus are collectively called nucleons.

Section 8.7 presents the Bohr-Bury rules for distributing electrons among shells: the maximum number of electrons in any shell is given by 2n², giving 2 for the K-shell, 8 for the L-shell, and 18 for the M-shell; the outermost shell can hold a maximum of 8 electrons; shells fill sequentially from the innermost outward. The section works through hydrogen as a starting example, with its single electron occupying the K-shell.

Section 8.7.1 builds on these rules by constructing two-dimensional atomic structures for the first 18 elements, element by element, adding one electron each time the atomic number increases by 1. Section 8.8 introduces valency as the combining capacity of an atom — the number of hydrogen or chlorine atoms with which one atom of an element can combine to form a compound. The outermost shell is called the valence shell and its electrons are the valence electrons; a full outer shell of 8 electrons (octet) or 2 electrons (for helium) makes an atom stable and largely unreactive. Atoms with incomplete valence shells are reactive and achieve stability by losing electrons (if fewer than 4 valence electrons, e.g. sodium: config 2, 8, 1; valency 1), gaining electrons (if more than 4, e.g. oxygen: config 2, 6; valency 2), or sharing electrons (if exactly 4, e.g. carbon: config 2, 4; valency 4). Section 8.9 signals that the chapter will now go deeper into atomic structure, leading into the subsections on isotopes and isobars.

Section 8.9.1 introduces isotopes — atoms of the same element with the same atomic number (Z) but different mass numbers (A), arising from different numbers of neutrons. Dalton had assumed all atoms of an element are identical, but this was found to be incorrect. Hydrogen has three naturally occurring isotopes: protium (¹₁H, ~99.98%), deuterium (²₁H, ~0.015%), and tritium (³₁H, in traces). Carbon has three isotopes: ¹²₆C (most abundant), ¹³₆C, and ¹⁴₆C — each with six protons and six electrons but differing in neutron count. Isotopes share the same chemical properties (same electronic configuration and valence electrons) but differ in physical properties such as boiling and melting points.

Named content-block A (Average atomic mass) explains that since isotopes occur in unequal natural abundances, atomic mass is reported as a weighted average atomic mass rather than a simple arithmetic mean. Using chlorine as the worked example: ³⁵Cl (~75%) and ³⁷Cl (~25%) give a simple average of 36 u, but the weighted average is (35 × 75/100) + (37 × 25/100) = 35.5 u. This weighted value accurately reflects the mass of chlorine as it occurs in nature; no individual chlorine atom has a fractional mass — the value represents the statistical average across a large population of atoms.

Section 8.9.2 introduces isobars as the counterpart to isotopes: atoms of different elements that share the same mass number but have different atomic numbers. The example given is calcium (Z = 20), potassium (Z = 19), and argon (Z = 18), all with mass number 40. The chapter closes by noting that even Bohr's model was later found to be incomplete — electrons do not follow fixed paths but exist as electron clouds around the nucleus, with positions describable only as probability distributions; the full quantum mechanical model will be studied in higher grades.

The chapter also connects atomic science to India's research capabilities and to practical societal applications. The Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC), Mumbai, conducts advanced neutron-scattering experiments using the Dhruva reactor, yielding insights into superconductors, battery electrodes, and drug molecules — contributing to medicines, energy storage, and industrial alloys. Several isotopes have vital real-world uses: ²³⁵₉₂U fuels nuclear reactors for electricity generation; ⁶⁰₂₇Co is used in cancer radiation therapy; ¹³¹₅₃I treats goitre and thyroid cancer; and ¹⁴₆C is used in radiocarbon dating of ancient fossils and artefacts.
